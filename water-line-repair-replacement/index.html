The main water service line running from the street to a Mechanicsburg home is easy to overlook until it fails.

It usually shows up as a sudden drop in pressure across the whole house or an unexplained wet patch in the yard along its path. Whether that means a spot repair or a full line replacement depends on the pipe material and how many separate failure points a camera inspection turns up.

Know The Signs.

Is Your Water Service Line Failing?

Whole-House Pressure Drop.

A sudden, sitewide pressure loss, not just at one fixture, often points to the main service line rather than internal plumbing.

A Wet Or Sunken Patch In The Yard.

Especially along the likely path between the street and the house, can mean an underground leak.

Discolored Water Sitewide.

Rust-colored water from every fixture at once suggests corrosion inside an aging galvanized service line.

Unusually High Water Bill.

A slow underground leak can run for weeks before becoming visible any other way.

Repair Options.

Spot Repair vs. Full Replacement

Spot Repair.

  • Targets a single confirmed failure point.
  • Lower cost and faster for an otherwise sound line.
  • Best on a newer line with one isolated issue.

Full Trenchless Replacement.

  • Replaces the entire line without a full open trench.
  • The standard fix once multiple failure points show up.
  • Recommended for original galvanized lines nearing the end of their working life.

Homes in and around the Mechanicsburg Commercial Historic District still running an original galvanized service line are more likely to need a full replacement rather than a spot repair. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside along its entire length, not just at one point. A single visible failure often signals the rest of the line is close behind it, and often means the interior supply lines are due for the same treatment around the same time.

The water meter itself is worth checking as part of any water-line diagnosis, since a meter with a visible leak indicator still spinning after every fixture is shut off points to a leak somewhere between the meter and the house, not a fixture problem inside. This is the same kind of moving meter with everything off that general leak detection checks for, just applied to the main service line specifically. Utility-side lines, from the street main to the meter, are typically the water provider's responsibility to repair. The line from the meter to the house is the homeowner's, a distinction worth confirming with the utility before assuming a repair cost falls entirely on one side.

Pipe material affects far more than corrosion risk alone. A copper service line, while more corrosion resistant than galvanized, can still develop pinhole leaks in certain soil conditions over several decades, the same failure pattern behind many slab leaks found under a home's foundation rather than in the yard. Modern PEX or HDPE lines used in newer construction resist both corrosion and the ground movement that eventually cracks more rigid pipe materials. Knowing the material of an existing line, often noted on the original building permit or discoverable during the camera inspection, shapes both the realistic lifespan expectation and the recommended repair approach when something does go wrong.
