A garbage disposal that hums but will not spin is usually jammed, not dead, and a leak from the bottom of the unit almost always means it needs replacement rather than repair.

Both are common calls in Mechanicsburg kitchens, and telling the two apart before a technician arrives helps set the right expectation for cost and timeline.

Know The Signs.

Jammed, Leaking, Or Dead

Humming But Not Spinning.

Almost always a jam, clearable with the reset button and the hex-key manual turn tool most units include.

No Sound At All.

Could be a tripped reset button, a dead outlet, or a failed motor, worth a quick check before assuming the worst.

Leaking From The Bottom.

Usually means the internal seal has failed, which is not repairable, and the unit needs replacement.

Leaking From The Top, Near The Sink Flange.

Often just a worn flange seal, a straightforward and inexpensive repair rather than a full replacement.

What Not To Do.

Common Mistakes That Make It Worse

Reaching into a jammed disposal by hand, even with the power supposedly off, risks serious injury if the unit is accidentally triggered. The manual turn tool included with most units, inserted into the bottom center hex socket, safely frees a jam without ever putting a hand near the blades. Pouring grease down the disposal is the other common mistake: it coats the chamber and blades the same way it coats drain pipes, contributing to both jams and eventual motor strain.

A disposal that backs up water into the sink when running is often actually a downstream drain clog rather than a disposal malfunction, since the unit itself has nowhere to send the water it is processing. A leak from the disposal's bottom seam worth ruling out alongside a check of the faucet and sink supply lines sharing the same cabinet space, since both fail from similar age and use patterns.

A disposal that trips a circuit breaker rather than just its own reset button points to a different problem, either a genuine electrical fault in the unit or wiring that was undersized for the horsepower being installed. That distinction matters for installation quotes on higher-horsepower replacement units, since an older kitchen circuit sized for a modest half-horsepower disposal does not always support a one-horsepower unit without additional electrical work. Confirming the existing circuit's capacity before selecting a replacement unit avoids a repeat service call for what looks like a plumbing problem but is actually electrical.

Certain foods are worth keeping out of any disposal regardless of horsepower. Fibrous items like celery and corn husks wrap around the blade assembly rather than grinding cleanly, and hard items like fruit pits or bones can chip the blades over time even on a heavy-duty unit. A disposal treated as a garbage chute rather than a tool for small food scraps tends to need repair or replacement noticeably sooner than one used within its intended limits. Persistent buildup that survives normal use often ends up as a drain line issue further downstream rather than something the disposal itself can be blamed for indefinitely.

Running cold water, not hot, while the disposal operates is worth doing consistently rather than only when remembered. Cold water keeps any grease that enters the unit solid enough to be chopped and flushed through. Melted grease coats the blades and chamber walls instead, and over months that buildup becomes the same kind of problem that eventually causes a jam or a persistent odor even when the unit is technically still working.
