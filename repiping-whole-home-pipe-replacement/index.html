A pattern of repeated leaks in different spots around the house, rather than one isolated failure, is usually what brings Mechanicsburg homeowners to consider a full repipe rather than another individual spot repair.

Original galvanized supply lines, common in homes built before the 1960s in and around the historic district, corrode from the inside along their entire length, which is why chasing individual leaks on an old system eventually costs more than replacing it outright.

Know The Signs.

Is It Time To Repipe?

Repeated Leaks In Different Locations.

A pattern rather than a single incident is the clearest sign the whole system, not one section, is failing.

Rust-Colored Water Sitewide.

Internal corrosion throughout galvanized pipe showing up at every fixture, not just one.

Noticeably Low Pressure Throughout The House.

Corrosion narrowing the interior diameter of galvanized pipe over decades restricts flow gradually.

Pipes Original To A Pre-1960s Home.

Original galvanized supply lines are well past their expected working life at this point regardless of visible symptoms yet.

Minimizing Disruption.

How Repiping Is Done With Minimal Wall Damage

Modern repiping techniques route new PEX or copper lines through existing wall cavities, attic, and crawlspace access wherever possible, opening small, targeted sections of drywall rather than tearing out entire walls. A phased approach, tackling one section of the house at a time, is also an option for a household that needs to keep water running to most of the home throughout the project rather than losing it all at once.

PEX vs. Copper.

Choosing A Replacement Material

Copper.

  • Long track record and high durability.
  • Higher material and labor cost.
  • More rigid, requiring more precise routing.

PEX.

  • Flexible, faster to install through existing cavities.
  • Lower material cost than copper.
  • Resistant to the corrosion that affects both copper and galvanized pipe.

Drain, waste, and vent piping is a separate consideration from supply line repiping, worth mentioning since the two are sometimes confused. Old cast-iron drain lines can remain structurally sound for decades longer than the galvanized supply lines running alongside them. That is why a full-home project sometimes replaces supply lines only, leaving drain piping in place after a camera inspection confirms it does not need replacement at the same time.

A repipe project is also a natural point to address any known low-pressure complaints tied to undersized original piping rather than corrosion alone. Homes built when a single bathroom was standard sometimes have main supply lines sized for far less simultaneous fixture use than a household runs today. Upsizing the main trunk line during a repipe, while the walls are already open, avoids paying for that access twice if pressure complaints were to be addressed as a separate project later. A household also planning a bathroom renovation around the same time should coordinate both projects, since fixture rough-in and repiping both require the same wall access.

Shutoff valves at every fixture are usually replaced as part of a full repipe even though they are not strictly part of the supply line itself. Old, corroded shutoffs are one of the more common reasons a simple future faucet or toilet repair turns into an unplanned emergency call. Fresh shutoffs at every fixture mean any future repair starts with a valve that actually closes, rather than one seized open from years of mineral buildup.

Common Questions.

Straight Answers

Most single-family homes are completed in 2 to 5 days depending on size and material choice, with water restored to most fixtures well before the full project, including drywall patching, is finished.

Most homeowners stay in the home throughout, though water is shut off during active work hours on the section being repiped that day, restored each evening in most cases.

PEX has a strong multi-decade track record at this point and resists the corrosion that eventually affects both copper and galvanized pipe, though copper remains a reasonable choice for a homeowner who prefers its longer overall history.
