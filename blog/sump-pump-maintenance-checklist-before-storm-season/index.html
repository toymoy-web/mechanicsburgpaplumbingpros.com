Sump Pump Maintenance Checklist Before Storm Season.

By the Mechanicsburg Plumbing Pros team.

A sump pump that has sat unused for months is exactly the kind of equipment that fails silently, showing no problem until the moment it is actually needed during a major storm.

A handful of checks, run once before storm season starts in the Cumberland Valley, catch most of the common failure points before they turn into a flooded basement.

The Bucket Test.

The single most useful test takes under five minutes. Pour a few gallons of water directly into the sump pit and watch what happens. The float switch should rise with the water level, the pump should kick on within a few seconds, and it should clear the water down to its normal resting level before shutting back off. A pump that hesitates, runs but does not actually move much water, or fails to shut off cleanly is telling you something specific about which component needs attention.

Checking The Discharge Line.

A pump can be working perfectly and still fail to protect a basement if the discharge line, the pipe carrying water away from the pump and out of the house, is blocked or frozen. Walk the visible length of the discharge line outside the house and confirm the outlet is clear of debris, ice, or a bird's nest, all of which are more common blockages than most homeowners expect. The outlet should also be positioned well away from the foundation, since a discharge line dumping water right back next to the house defeats much of the pump's purpose.

Testing The Backup Power.

If a battery backup system is installed, storm season is the time to confirm the battery actually holds a charge, not just that the indicator light looks normal. Most battery backup units have a test button or a documented way to simulate a power outage; running that test confirms the backup will actually take over rather than assuming it will based on age or a general sense that it was working recently. A battery more than 3 to 4 years old is worth replacing proactively rather than waiting for it to fail during the exact outage it exists to handle.

Listening For Warning Signs.

A pump that has started making a new grinding, rattling, or straining sound compared to how it normally runs is usually signaling a failing motor bearing or an impeller catching on debris in the pit. Either issue tends to get worse gradually rather than resolve on its own, which makes an unusual noise worth addressing before storm season rather than during the middle of a major rain event.

A sump pump inspection or repair catches most of these issues in a single visit. For a pump nearing the end of its typical 7 to 10 year working life, replacement before it fails is almost always less disruptive than an emergency call during an active storm. Pairing this checklist with a look at the drainage system feeding the pump makes sense too, since a pump can only move the water the drainage system actually routes to it.

This checklist matters most in Hampden Township, where the Conodoguinet Creek's repeated bends through the middle of the township keep certain low-lying streets at higher risk of a high water table well before storm season even begins.

What Not To Overlook: The Check Valve.

A check valve sits just above the pump and prevents water already pumped out from flowing back down into the pit once the pump shuts off. A missing, backward-installed, or failed check valve forces the pump to immediately re-pump the same water that just drained back in, which shows up as a pump cycling far more often than the actual inflow would require. This is one of the more commonly overlooked components during a casual self-check, since it sits inside the discharge pipe rather than somewhere visibly obvious, but a technician can confirm it is functioning correctly as part of a full inspection.

A Note On Pit Size And Pump Sizing.

A sump pit that is too small for the volume of water a property's drainage system actually delivers forces the pump to cycle on and off rapidly rather than running efficiently for a sustained period. That rapid cycling adds wear beyond what the pump's rated lifespan assumes, which is one of the less obvious reasons a pump might fail earlier than expected even with otherwise normal maintenance. Confirming that the pit and pump were sized appropriately for the property, not just installed to a generic residential default, is worth doing once as part of a broader system check rather than assuming the original installation got it right.

What A Full Service Visit Adds Beyond A Self-Check.

A homeowner's own bucket test and visual check catch the most obvious failure points. A full technician visit typically also tests the pump under a simulated heavy-inflow scenario. It checks the motor's amperage draw against its rated specification to catch early motor wear before it causes an outright failure, and confirms the float switch travels its full range without catching on the pit's wall or any debris inside it. That level of detail is difficult to replicate with a home test alone, which is why an annual professional check is worth pairing with the more frequent self-checks a homeowner can run.
