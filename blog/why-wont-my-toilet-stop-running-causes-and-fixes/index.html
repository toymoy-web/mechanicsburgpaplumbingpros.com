Why Won't My Toilet Stop Running? Common Causes And Fixes.

By the Mechanicsburg Plumbing Pros team.

A toilet that keeps running, or that briefly refills every few minutes on its own, is one of the most common calls in any plumbing business, and it is almost always one of a small handful of specific causes rather than a mystery.

Working through them in order, from most to least common, resolves most running toilets without needing to replace the whole fixture.

The Flapper: The Most Common Cause By Far.

The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that lifts when the handle is pressed, letting water flow into the bowl, then drops back down to seal the tank. Over time, mineral deposits and rubber degradation prevent it from sealing completely, letting water slowly leak from the tank into the bowl, which triggers the fill valve to keep topping off the tank. A flapper showing any warping, cracking, or mineral buildup along its sealing edge is due for replacement, an inexpensive and straightforward fix.

The Fill Valve: The Second Most Common Cause.

If the flapper looks fine and seals properly, the fill valve itself may not be shutting off completely once the tank reaches its target level. This shows up as water continuously trickling into the overflow tube rather than stopping, sometimes audible as a faint hissing sound. Fill valves are also relatively inexpensive to replace and are a common wear item on an older toilet.

The Float: Often Overlooked.

The float, either a ball on an arm or a cylinder around the fill valve depending on the toilet's age and design, tells the fill valve when to stop filling. A float set too high causes water to constantly spill into the overflow tube even with a functioning fill valve and flapper. Adjusting the float height, usually a simple screw or clip adjustment, sometimes resolves a running toilet that looked like it needed new parts.

A Cracked Overflow Tube Or Tank.

Less common but worth ruling out on an older toilet, a crack in the overflow tube itself or in the tank lets water escape in a way none of the above fixes address. This usually requires either a tank replacement or, in some cases, a full toilet replacement depending on the tank's age and the availability of matching parts.

Most running toilet calls are resolved with a straightforward flapper, fill valve, or float repair, typically completed in a single visit. A toilet showing water pooling around its base, rather than just running internally, points to a different problem, a failed wax ring seal, which is a related but distinct issue worth mentioning during the same call if it applies. Faucet issues sometimes get lumped in with toilet complaints during the same visit too, and faucet and sink repair covers the similarly common drip-from-a-worn-cartridge problem using the same basic diagnostic approach: identify the specific worn component before assuming the whole fixture needs replacing.

This kind of straightforward fixture repair comes up constantly in Camp Hill, where the historic district's older toilets and faucets see more wear-related calls than the newer fixtures common in more recently built neighborhoods.

How Much A Running Toilet Actually Costs.

A toilet running continuously can waste hundreds of gallons a day, which adds up to a real, measurable increase on a water bill within a single billing cycle. Unlike a hidden leak, this waste is usually audible or visible enough that most households notice something is wrong well before the bill arrives, but it is common to put off the fix for weeks simply because the toilet still technically works. That delay adds real, avoidable cost for a repair that is almost always inexpensive and quick once it is actually addressed.

When A Repair Becomes A Replacement Decision.

A toilet older than 20 years, particularly a pre-1994 model built before current water-efficiency standards, is sometimes better replaced than repeatedly repaired, since these older models use significantly more water per flush regardless of how well their internal components are maintained. A newer, water-efficient toilet often pays back its replacement cost through lower water bills over time, separate from whatever repair costs get avoided by not chasing recurring issues on an aging fixture.

Preventing The Problem From Recurring.

Hard water mineral buildup is a major contributor to flapper and fill valve wear in this region specifically, since scale deposits along the sealing edges prevent a complete seal even on a relatively new part. A toilet in a household already dealing with hard water elsewhere in the home is statistically more likely to develop this exact problem again within a few years. That is one more reason addressing whole-home water hardness tends to reduce a whole category of recurring fixture complaints, not just this one.
