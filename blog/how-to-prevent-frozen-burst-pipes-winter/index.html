How to Prevent Frozen & Burst Pipes This Winter.

By the Mechanicsburg Plumbing Pros team.

Burst pipe calls in Mechanicsburg do not spread evenly across the year. They cluster hard between December and March, and the reason has less to do with how cold it gets and more to do with how often the temperature swings back and forth across freezing.

A pipe that freezes once and stays frozen is actually less likely to burst than one that freezes, partially thaws, and refreezes several times in a week, which is exactly the pattern the Cumberland Valley sees most winters.

Why Freeze-Thaw Cycles Are Worse Than A Steady Cold Snap.

Water expands by about 9 percent as it freezes, and that expansion is what puts pressure on a pipe wall from the inside. A single hard freeze that stays frozen for days puts steady, predictable stress on a line. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles do something worse: each freeze expands the ice, each partial thaw lets water shift and refill the space, and each refreeze expands it again against a slightly different section of pipe wall. That repeated flexing is what eventually cracks copper or splits a joint, more than raw cold ever does on its own.

The Pipes Most At Risk.

Not every pipe in a house faces the same risk. Lines running through unheated spaces, crawlspaces, attics, garages, and along exterior walls with poor insulation, take the brunt of it. Older homes in and around Cumberland County's historic boroughs often have supply lines that were never insulated to modern standards when they were first run, simply because insulation requirements looked different decades ago. A pipe running along an exterior wall in an addition or a converted porch is a common trouble spot for exactly this reason.

What Actually Works.

Pipe insulation sleeves on exposed lines in unheated spaces are the most direct fix, and they are inexpensive relative to a burst-pipe repair. Sealing air leaks around where pipes pass through exterior walls or foundation sills matters just as much as insulating the pipe itself, since cold air infiltration is often the actual source of the freeze, not just ambient outdoor temperature. Keeping cabinet doors open under sinks on exterior walls during a hard cold snap lets household heat reach pipes that would otherwise sit in a sealed, unheated pocket of cabinet space. A trickle of water left running from a faucet fed by an at-risk line also helps, since moving water is harder to freeze solid than standing water.

If A Pipe Has Already Frozen.

A frozen but not yet burst pipe still gives a window to act. Turn off the main water valve first, since a frozen section that thaws while still under full pressure is a common trigger for the actual burst. Open the affected faucet to relieve pressure as the line thaws. A hair dryer or a space heater held a safe distance away can gently warm an exposed section; an open flame should never be used near a pipe, regardless of the material. If the frozen section is not visible or accessible, or if water is already escaping anywhere, that is the point to call rather than keep troubleshooting.

Homes still running original galvanized or aging copper supply lines are especially vulnerable to this repeated-cycle stress, since decades of prior wear leave less margin before a crack forms. If a burst does happen despite prevention efforts, emergency repair focuses first on stopping the water, then on checking the full run for additional weak points before calling the job done.

Shiremanstown's older, densely built housing stock is a good example of where this risk concentrates. Much of the borough is still on original galvanized supply lines from before 1960, which means a freeze-thaw winter hits harder there than in a newer-construction neighborhood running modern PEX.

A Note On Outdoor Fixtures.

Outdoor hose bibs and irrigation lines are worth a separate mention, since they fail through a slightly different mechanism than interior supply lines. A standard hose bib, even a modern frost-proof model, can still freeze and crack if a hose is left attached over the winter, since the attached hose traps water inside the valve body that would otherwise drain out. Disconnecting and draining every garden hose before the first hard freeze, and shutting off any dedicated interior valve that feeds an outdoor spigot, are two of the simplest and most commonly skipped steps in a fall winterization routine. An irrigation system needs a full professional blow-out with compressed air in most cases, since residual water sitting in underground lines will not drain on its own and freezing there can crack both the pipe and any valves along the line.

What A Realistic Prevention Routine Looks Like.

Rather than treating pipe protection as a single task done once in the fall, the households that avoid burst pipes most consistently treat it as an ongoing check throughout the coldest months. That means paying attention to the forecast for genuinely hard freezes, not just any night below freezing. It also means taking the extra precautions, cabinet doors open, a faucet trickling, an eye on any known trouble spot, specifically during those colder snaps rather than as a one-time fall chore. A home with a known history of a frozen pipe in a specific location benefits from a permanent fix, better insulation or pipe rerouting, rather than repeating the same seasonal workaround year after year.
