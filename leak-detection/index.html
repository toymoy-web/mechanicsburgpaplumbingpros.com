Most leak detection calls in Mechanicsburg start the same way: a water bill that jumped for no obvious reason, or a meter that keeps spinning with every fixture in the house turned off. There is rarely a visible puddle to point to, which is exactly why guessing at the source and cutting into a wall or slab on a hunch is the wrong first move.

Electronic detection finds the leak's actual location first, so any repair that follows opens up the smallest area possible instead of the largest one.

Know The Signs.

You Might Have A Hidden Leak If

The Water Bill Jumped

A bill noticeably higher than the same month last year, with no change in household water use.

The Meter Runs With Everything Off

Shut off every fixture in the house. If the meter's leak indicator still moves, water is going somewhere in the system.

A Warm Spot On The Floor

Often points to a hot water line leaking under a slab, even with no visible moisture yet.

A Sound Behind The Wall

A faint hiss or running-water sound with no fixture in use nearby is worth investigating before it becomes visible damage.

How It Works.

Finding A Leak Without Guessing

Electronic leak detection uses acoustic listening equipment and, where needed, thermal imaging to trace a leak's actual location through walls, slabs, and underground lines before anyone opens anything up. The alternative, cutting into drywall or a slab based on where the damage happens to be visible, often misses the actual source, since water travels along pipe runs and structural gaps before it ever shows up on a ceiling or floor.

After The Leak Is Found.

Repair Options, Honestly Compared

Full Slab Excavation.

  • Higher cost and longer repair time.
  • More disruption to flooring/finished space.
  • Sometimes the only option for an older, failing line.

Spot Repair Or Reroute.

  • Targets only the failed section.
  • Faster turnaround for most single-point leaks.
  • Standard fix once the leak is precisely located.

A confirmed slab leak and a leak in an accessible wall cavity are repaired differently, which is exactly why locating it precisely first changes the whole scope and cost of the job that follows, not just where the crew starts cutting.

Not every burst is obvious at first. A steady, unexplained drop in water pressure sitewide sometimes means a hidden failure further down the main water service line rather than a burst inside the house, which changes both the diagnosis and the repair approach. A leak found near a water heater, rather than in an open wall or slab, sometimes points to the unit itself failing rather than the supply piping around it.

Where This Shows Up Most.

Older Service Lines, Higher Risk

Homes built before the 1970s in and around the Mechanicsburg Commercial Historic District are more likely to still have the original copper or galvanized service line running from the street to the house. Those lines corrode from the inside out over decades, and a pinhole leak in a buried section can run for months before the water bill jumps enough to notice. Newer construction in the townships around Mechanicsburg uses PEX and copper with far fewer of these slow underground failures, which is part of why the home's age is one of the first questions worth asking on the phone before a technician is even dispatched.

Most standard homeowner insurance treats a slow, long-term leak as a maintenance issue rather than a covered loss, which is different from the sudden-and-accidental coverage that applies to a burst pipe. Catching a hidden leak early, before it turns into a covered emergency, is usually worth more than the cost of the detection visit itself.
